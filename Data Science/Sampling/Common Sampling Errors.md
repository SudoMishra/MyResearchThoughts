
### [[Selection Bias]]

When the sample selection process systematically favors certain outcomes.

**Example:** A telephone survey that only calls landlines would systematically exclude people who only use mobile phones, potentially overrepresenting older demographic groups and missing younger respondents' perspectives.

### [[Undercoverage]]

When portions of the population have zero or reduced chances of being sampled.

**Example:** An online survey about internet usage habits would necessarily exclude people without internet access, ironically missing data from the very group that might be most affected by digital divides.

### [[Survivorship Bias]]

Drawing conclusions based only on "survivors" of a selection process.

**Example:** Studying only successful businesses to identify factors for entrepreneurial success overlooks failed businesses, which might reveal equally important lessons about what practices to avoid.

## [[Advanced Sampling Methods]]

### [[Adaptive Sampling]]

Sampling methods that adjust based on what is learned during data collection.

**Example:** Wildlife researchers initially place camera traps in a grid pattern across a forest to monitor a rare species. After identifying areas with higher activity, they adjust their sampling to focus more resources on these promising locations.

### [[Sequential Sampling]]

Data collection continues until a threshold condition is met.

**Example:** A quality control process keeps testing product units until either 10 defects are found (indicating a problem) or 100 consecutive units pass inspection (indicating acceptable quality).

These methods and objectives help ensure that the data we collect provides a solid foundation for analysis and decision-making, balancing statistical validity with practical constraints.
