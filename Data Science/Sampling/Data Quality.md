# Data Sampling Methods and Objectives

Let's explore the various methods and objectives for sampling data to ensure good quality data collection.

## Sampling Objectives

The primary goals of proper sampling include:

- [[Representative sampling]] - Ensuring the sample accurately reflects the characteristics of the population
- [[Minimizing bias]] - Reducing systematic errors that could skew results
- [[Statistical validity]] - Enabling reliable statistical inference and generalization
- [[Resource optimization]] - Balancing accuracy with practical constraints like time and cost

## Probability Sampling Methods

### [[Simple Random Sampling]]

In simple random sampling, each member of the population has an equal chance of being selected.

**Example:** A researcher studying employee satisfaction at a company with 500 employees assigns each employee a number from 1-500, then uses a random number generator to select 50 employees for the survey. This approach gives every employee the same probability (10%) of being included in the sample.

### [[Stratified Sampling]]

The population is divided into distinct subgroups (strata) before sampling, ensuring representation from each important segment.

**Example:** When studying voter preferences in a country, the researcher might divide the population into geographic regions (urban, suburban, rural) and then randomly sample within each region. This ensures that the final sample includes adequate representation from each regional category, which might be crucial for understanding voting patterns.

### [[Cluster Sampling]]

The population is divided into clusters (often based on geography), and entire clusters are randomly selected.

**Example:** A health department wants to assess vaccination rates across a large city. Instead of sampling individual households throughout the city, they randomly select 20 city blocks (clusters) and survey every household within those selected blocks. This method is particularly efficient when face-to-face data collection is required.

### [[Systematic Sampling]]

Items are selected at regular intervals after a random start point.

**Example:** A quality control inspector wants to check products coming off an assembly line. Rather than testing every item (too time-consuming) or picking completely at random, they might test every 10th item after randomly selecting a starting point between 1 and 10. This creates a systematic pattern while maintaining randomness.

## Non-Probability Sampling Methods

### [[Convenience Sampling]]

Samples are selected based on ease of access rather than randomization.

**Example:** A store places a customer satisfaction survey near its exit, allowing customers to participate voluntarily. While convenient, this method may oversample customers with strong opinions (positive or negative) and miss those who are indifferent.

### [[Purposive Sampling]]

Samples are deliberately chosen based on specific characteristics.

**Example:** A researcher studying the leadership challenges of female executives in tech companies specifically recruits women in C-suite positions at technology firms. This targeted approach ensures the sample consists precisely of the population of interest.

### [[Snowball Sampling]]

Initial participants recruit additional participants from their networks.

**Example:** When researching a rare medical condition, researchers might identify a few patients with the condition and ask them to refer other patients they know. This approach is valuable for studying hard-to-reach populations but may suffer from community bias.

### [[Quota Sampling]]

Researchers set quotas for specific population segments to ensure their representation.

**Example:** A market research firm conducting a product preference study might set quotas to ensure their sample includes specific numbers of participants across different age groups, income levels, and genders, making the sample demographically similar to their target market.

## [[Sample Size Determination]]

The appropriate sample size depends on:

- Required confidence level
- Acceptable margin of error
- Population variance
- Population size (for small populations)

**Example:** A political polling organization wants to predict election results with a 95% confidence level and ±3% margin of error. Using statistical formulas that incorporate these parameters and assumptions about population variance, they determine they need to survey approximately 1,067 likely voters to achieve their precision goals.
